1. Introduction to Electrical Principles

1.1 Meaning of Electricity

Electricity is a form of energy associated with electric charges. It is used in lighting, communication, computing, transportation and many industrial applications.

1.2 Electric Charge

Electric charge is a fundamental property of matter responsible for electrical forces. There are two types of electric charge:

  • Positive charge.
  • Negative charge.

Like charges repel each other, while unlike charges attract.

1.3 Conductors and Insulators

Conductors Insulators
Allow electric current to flow relatively easily. Resist the flow of electric current.
Copper, aluminium and silver. Rubber, glass and dry plastic.

2. Electric Current

2.1 Definition

Electric current is the rate of flow of electric charge through a conductor.

I = Q / t
Where:
  • I = current in amperes (A).
  • Q = charge in coulombs (C).
  • t = time in seconds (s).

2.2 Worked Example

A charge of 20 C passes through a conductor in 5 seconds. Calculate the current. 

Given:
Q = 20 C
t = 5 s

I = Q / t
I = 20 / 5
I = 4 A

Answer: 4 amperes.

2.3 Types of Current

  • Direct Current (DC): Current that flows in one direction.
  • Alternating Current (AC): Current that periodically changes direction.

DC and AC Waveforms

DC remains in one direction. AC alternates between positive and negative values.

3. Voltage and Potential Difference

3.1 Definition

Voltage, also known as potential difference, is the energy transferred or work done per unit electric charge between two points.

V = W / Q
  • V = voltage in volts.
  • W = work done in joules.
  • Q = charge in coulombs.

3.2 Worked Example

If 60 joules of energy are transferred by a charge of 10 coulombs, calculate the voltage. 

V = W / Q
V = 60 / 10
V = 6 V

Answer: 6 volts.

4. Electrical Resistance

4.1 Definition

Resistance is the opposition offered by a material to the flow of electric current. Its SI unit is the ohm (Ω).

4.2 Factors Affecting Resistance

  • Length: Increasing the length generally increases resistance.
  • Cross-sectional area: Increasing the area generally reduces resistance.
  • Material: Different materials have different resistivities.
  • Temperature: Resistance changes with temperature depending on the material.

4.3 Resistance Formula

R = ρL / A
  • R = resistance in ohms.
  • ρ = resistivity in ohm metres (Ωm).
  • L = length in metres.
  • A = cross-sectional area in square metres.

4.4 Worked Example

A conductor has a resistivity of 2 × 10⁻⁶ Ωm, a length of 4 m and a cross-sectional area of 2 × 10⁻⁶ m². Find its resistance. 

R = ρL / A

R = (2 × 10⁻⁶ × 4) / (2 × 10⁻⁶)

R = 4 Ω

Answer: 4 ohms.

5. Ohm's Law

5.1 Statement

Ohm's law states that the current flowing through a conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across it, provided temperature and other physical conditions remain constant.

V = IR
  • V = voltage in volts.
  • I = current in amperes.
  • R = resistance in ohms.

5.2 Ohm's Law Triangle

Cover the quantity you want to calculate to identify the required formula.

The three forms are:

  • V = I × R
  • I = V / R
  • R = V / I

5.3 Worked Example

A resistor of 6 Ω is connected across a 12 V supply. Calculate the current. 

Given:
V = 12 V
R = 6 Ω

I = V / R
I = 12 / 6
I = 2 A

Answer: 2 amperes.

6. Electrical Circuits

6.1 Meaning of an Electric Circuit

An electric circuit is a complete conducting path through which electric current can flow. A simple circuit may contain a power source, connecting wires, a switch and a load.

6.2 Simple Electric Circuit

Basic Circuit Diagram

A closed circuit provides a complete path for current to flow.

6.3 Open and Closed Circuits

  • Closed circuit: The conducting path is complete and current can flow.
  • Open circuit: The path is broken and current does not flow.

7. Series Circuits

7.1 Definition

A series circuit is a circuit in which components are connected one after another, forming a single path for current.

Series Circuit Diagram

7.2 Characteristics of Series Circuits

  • The same current flows through every component.
  • The supply voltage is shared between components.
  • The total resistance is the sum of individual resistances.
  • If one component breaks the circuit, current stops flowing throughout the circuit.

7.3 Total Resistance in Series

Rₜ = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ + ...

7.4 Worked Example

Three resistors of 2 Ω, 4 Ω and 6 Ω are connected in series. Find the total resistance. 

Rₜ = R₁ + R₂ + R₃

Rₜ = 2 + 4 + 6

Rₜ = 12 Ω

Answer: 12 ohms.

8. Parallel Circuits

8.1 Definition

A parallel circuit is a circuit in which components are connected across the same two electrical nodes, providing separate paths for current.

Parallel Circuit Diagram

Each resistor has its own branch between the same two nodes.

8.2 Characteristics of Parallel Circuits

  • The voltage across each branch is the same.
  • The total current is the sum of the branch currents.
  • Current divides between the branches.
  • If one branch is interrupted, other branches may still operate.

8.3 Total Resistance in Parallel

1/Rₜ = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃ + ...

8.4 Worked Example

Two resistors of 6 Ω and 3 Ω are connected in parallel. Find their equivalent resistance. 

1/Rₜ = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂

1/Rₜ = 1/6 + 1/3

1/Rₜ = 1/6 + 2/6

1/Rₜ = 3/6 = 1/2

Rₜ = 2 Ω

Answer: 2 ohms.

9. Kirchhoff's Laws

9.1 Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL)

Kirchhoff's Current Law states that the total current entering a junction equals the total current leaving that junction.

Current at a Junction

Illustrative junction: the current values must satisfy conservation of charge.

Formula:
ΣI entering = ΣI leaving

9.2 Worked Example

A junction has 5 A entering and two currents of 2 A and 3 A leaving. Verify Kirchhoff's Current Law. 

Entering current = 5 A

Leaving current = 2 + 3
                = 5 A

Therefore:
5 A = 5 A

The law is satisfied.

9.3 Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL)

Kirchhoff's Voltage Law states that the algebraic sum of all voltage rises and voltage drops around a closed circuit is zero.

ΣV = 0

For a simple loop, the supply voltage equals the sum of the voltage drops across the components.

10. Electrical Power and Energy

10.1 Electrical Power

Electrical power is the rate at which electrical energy is transferred or converted.

P = VI
Other useful forms:
  • P = I²R
  • P = V²/R

10.2 Worked Example

A device operates at 12 V and draws a current of 2 A. Calculate its power. 

P = VI

P = 12 × 2

P = 24 W

Answer: 24 watts.

10.3 Electrical Energy

Electrical energy is the total amount of work done or energy transferred over a period of time.

E = Pt
  • E = energy in joules.
  • P = power in watts.
  • t = time in seconds.

10.4 Worked Example

A 100 W lamp operates for 10 seconds. Calculate the energy used. 

E = Pt

E = 100 × 10

E = 1000 J

Answer: 1000 joules.

16. Revision Summary

  • Electricity involves electric charges and their movement.
  • Current is the rate of flow of charge.
  • Voltage is energy transferred per unit charge.
  • Resistance opposes the flow of current.
  • Ohm's law relates voltage, current and resistance.
  • Series circuits have one current path.
  • Parallel circuits have separate branches.
  • Kirchhoff's laws describe current and voltage in circuits.
  • Electrical power is the rate of energy transfer.
